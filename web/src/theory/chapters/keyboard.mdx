<p class="lede">Every piece of music you've ever heard is built from a small set of notes, used over and over at different heights. The piano keyboard lays them all out in a row, so it's the clearest picture of them there is. Learn its pattern once and you can find any note, on any instrument.</p>

<Section id="pitch" title="Pitch: high and low">
  <p><Term>Pitch</Term> is how high or low a sound is. A bird's chirp is high; a bass guitar is low. On a keyboard, pitch goes up as you move right and down as you move left. Press a few keys below, from one end to the other, and listen to the pitch climb.</p>
  <Keyboard client:visible from={48} to={83} names="none" label="A three-octave keyboard to play" caption="Low on the left, high on the right." />
</Section>

<Section id="alphabet" title="The musical alphabet">
  <p>Notes are named with just seven letters: <b>A B C D E F G</b>. After G, the letters start again at A. That's the whole alphabet, and it repeats all the way up and down the keyboard.</p>
  <Big>A B C D E F G, then A again. Seven names, repeating forever.</Big>
  <p>The white keys are those seven letters in order. Most music starts its counting from C rather than A (you'll see why in the major scale chapter), so we'll read them as C D E F G A B.</p>
  <Keyboard client:visible from={60} to={83} names="white" label="Two octaves with every white key named C D E F G A B" />
</Section>

<Section id="pattern" title="The pattern of black keys">
  <p>The white keys all look the same, so how do you know which is C? The black keys tell you. They come in <b>groups of two</b> and{' '} <b>groups of three</b>, alternating all the way along.</p>
  <Groups client:visible />
  <p>Every note has a fixed place in that pattern. <b>C is the white key just left of each group of two.</b> Once you can see C, the rest follow: D sits <i>between</i> the two black keys, E just right of them. F is just left of the group of three, and G, A and B run through it.</p>
  <Try>
    <NoteFinder client:visible letter="C" />
  </Try>
  <Try title="Try another">
    <NoteFinder client:visible letter="F" />
  </Try>
  <Aside label="Landmarks">Two landmarks are worth learning cold: <b>C</b>, left of the two, and <b>F</b>, left of the three. Every other white key is one step from one of them.</Aside>
</Section>

<Section id="octaves" title="Octaves: the same note, higher">
  <p>Play a C, then the next C up. They sound different, one higher, and yet somehow the <i>same</i>. That's an <Term>octave</Term>: twelve keys up from any note, counting black and white, you reach the same letter again.</p>
  <Octaves client:visible />
  <p>Underneath, an octave is a doubling. A4, the A above middle C, vibrates 440 times a second; A5 vibrates 880 times. Your ear hears notes whose frequencies double as the same note, which is why the alphabet can repeat.</p>
  <p>To say <i>which</i> C, we add a number. The C near the middle of a piano is <Term>middle C</Term>, written <b>C4</b>. The next C up is C5, the one below is C3. The number changes at every C, so the B just below middle C is B3.</p>

<Section id="twelve" title="Twelve keys, then it repeats">
  <p>Count every key from one C up to (but not including) the next: seven white and five black, <b>twelve</b> in all. Those twelve are every pitch Western music uses. Everything else in this book, from scales and chords to whole songs, is a way of choosing from those twelve.</p>
  <Aside label="Black keys' names">The black keys have names too, borrowed from the white keys beside them: the one between C and D is C sharp or D flat. That's the next chapter.</Aside>
</Section>
