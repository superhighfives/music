<p class="lede">The last chapter gave every white key a name. This one measures the space between keys, and names the black keys too. Those small distances, the half step and the whole step, are the bricks every scale and chord is built from.</p>

<Section id="half-step" title="The half step">
  <p>A <Term>half step</Term> is the distance from one key to the very next key, black or white, with nothing in between. It's the smallest distance on a keyboard, and in most Western music. You'll also hear it called a <Term>semitone</Term>.</p>
  <Keyboard client:visible
    from={60}

    label="C to C sharp: one half step, from a white key to the black key right next to it"
    caption="C to the black key just right of it: one half step."
  />
  <Big>A half step is one key to the very next key. Nothing in between.</Big>
  <Chromatic client:visible />
</Section>

<Section id="no-black-key" title="Where there's no black key">
  <p>Look at the gaps in the pattern of black keys. Between <b>E and F</b>, and between <b>B and C</b>, there's no black key at all. Those white keys sit right next to each other, so they're only a half step apart.</p>
  <Keyboard client:visible
    from={60}
    to={72}
    names="white"
    marks={{ 64: { tone: 'lit' }, 65: { tone: 'lit' }, 71: { tone: 'cool' }, 72: { tone: 'cool' } }}
    arcs={[
      { from: 64, to: 65, label: '½', tone: 'lit' },
      { from: 71, to: 72, label: '½' },
    ]}
    label="E to F and B to C: white keys a half step apart, with no black key between"
    caption="E–F and B–C are the two pairs of white keys a half step apart."
  />
  <p>Every other pair of neighbouring white keys has a black key between them, so they're two half steps apart. That unevenness is not a flaw: it's what gives the major scale its shape, as you'll see in a later chapter.</p>
</Section>

<Section id="whole-step" title="The whole step">
  <p>Two half steps make a <Term>whole step</Term>, also called a <Term>tone</Term>. From C, a whole step up skips the black key and lands on D. From E, a whole step up skips F and lands on the black key above it.</p>
  <Keyboard client:visible
    from={60}
    to={71}
    names="white"

  <Aside label="Count keys, not colours">The easy mistake is to count white keys only. E to F looks like a whole step because they're two different white keys, but it's a half step. Always count every key you pass.</Aside>
</Section>

<Section id="accidentals" title="Sharps, flats and naturals">
  <p>The black keys borrow their names from the white keys beside them, using signs called <Term>accidentals</Term>:</p>
  <ul>
    <li>
      A <b>sharp ♯</b> raises a note by a half step. C♯ is the key just above C.
    </li>
    <li>
      A <b>flat ♭</b> lowers a note by a half step. D♭ is the key just below D.
    </li>
    <li>
      A <b>natural ♮</b> cancels a sharp or flat, back to the plain letter.
    </li>
  </ul>

<Section id="enharmonics" title="One key, two names">
  <p>So the black key between C and D is both C♯ and D♭. Two names for the same sound are called <Term>enharmonic</Term> spellings. Play C♯ and D♭ on a piano and it's the very same key.</p>
  <Keyboard client:visible
    from={60}
    to={71}
    names="white"
    marks={{ 61: { tone: 'lit', label: 'C♯' }, 63: { tone: 'cool', label: 'E♭' }, 66: { tone: 'lit', label: 'F♯' }, 70: { tone: 'cool', label: 'B♭' } }}
    label="Black keys labelled C sharp, E flat, F sharp and B flat"
    caption="C♯ is also D♭, E♭ is also D♯, F♯ is also G♭, B♭ is also A♯."
  />
  <p>White keys can have second names too. Since E to F is a half step, raising E gives you F: <b>E♯ is F</b>. Lowering C gives you B:{' '} <b>C♭ is B</b>. These look strange at first, and they're rare, but they turn up in keys with lots of sharps or flats.</p>
</Section>

<Section id="spelling" title="Why the name matters">
  <p>If C♯ and D♭ sound the same, why keep both names? Because the name tells you what the note is <i>doing</i>. In a scale, each letter is used once, so the same key gets a different name depending on the notes around it: in the key of D major it's C♯, and in A♭ major it's D♭.</p>
  <p>Think of it like spelling: <i>their</i> and <i>there</i> sound the same but mean different things. Choosing the right spelling makes music far easier to read, and you'll lean on it in the chapters on scales and intervals.</p>
  <Aside label="Coming up">The next chapter puts all of this on paper: the staff, where every letter has its own line or space, and accidentals sit just in front of the note.</Aside>
</Section>
